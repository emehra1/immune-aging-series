The case

Why immune aging,
why now

Written September 2026 · Cambridge, Massachusetts

The field spent a decade building toward one clean test of its central claim. In July 2026 it got the answer, and the answer was no. That is exactly why this is worth three evenings.

§ 01

The thing that just happened

For twenty years, the argument for treating inflammation as a driver of age-related disease rested on a single positive trial. CANTOS showed in 2017 that blocking one cytokine — interleukin-1β — reduced recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with residual inflammatory risk, without touching their cholesterol.1 It was the first prospective randomised proof that an inflammatory pathway causes a major age-related disease rather than merely marking it.

Everything downstream of that result — the biotech financings, the biomarker companies, the geroscience pitch decks — assumed CANTOS generalised. The obvious next move was to go one step down the pathway to interleukin-6, the cytokine that actually drives the hsCRP signal, with a cleaner drug and a better-selected population.

That trial was ZEUS. It enrolled 6,376 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and hsCRP at or above 2 mg/L — the enrichment strategy the field had spent a decade validating. Ziltivekimab did precisely what it was designed to do: free IL-6 fell, hsCRP fell, target engagement was never in question.

On 31 July 2026, Novo Nordisk reported the primary endpoint. Hazard ratio 0.99, 95% confidence interval 0.88–1.11.2 Serious infections were higher on drug. All-cause mortality was unchanged. The stock fell about 8.5% that day.

The biomarker moved perfectly and the patients did not benefit. That dissociation — not target validation — is now the field's central problem.

§ 02

What it did and did not kill

It is tempting to read ZEUS as the end of the inflammation hypothesis. It is not, and the reasons why are the substance of the first evening.

ZEUS enrolled an old population with badly compromised kidneys — mean age around 69, mean eGFR around 44, and 41% with existing heart failure. In patients like these, a large share of residual cardiovascular events comes from vascular calcification, arrhythmia and pump failure, none of which anti-inflammatory therapy would be expected to touch. A null result in that population is not the same as a null result in the biology.

There is also a structural question about where in the pathway to intervene. CANTOS blocked IL-1β, upstream, which also suppresses IL-18 and the rest of the inflammasome's output. ZEUS blocked IL-6, downstream, which suppresses the biomarker most cleanly but perhaps the least of the biology. Whether IL-6 is the causal node or merely the most legible readout of it is genuinely unresolved.

The tell that the field has not given up: in September 2025 Novartis paid $1.4B for Tourmaline Bio and its anti-IL-6 antibody pacibekitug. A month after ZEUS failed, at ESC 2026, they presented sustained biomarker data and confirmed they are proceeding to Phase 3 anyway.3

Either they know something about differentiated dosing and population selection that ZEUS did not test, or they are about to spend a great deal of money learning the same lesson twice. That is a question worth putting to a room.

§ 03

The other half of the field is having a much better year

While the inflammation programmes were failing, the reconstitution programmes were compounding. This is the asymmetry that makes a three-night structure necessary rather than decorative: suppressing inflammation and rebuilding immunity are different problems with different evidence bases, and 2026 pulled them apart.

In December 2025, Friedrich, Zhang and colleagues showed that transiently reprogramming the liver to secrete three trophic factors — DLL1, FLT3L and IL-7 — reignites thymopoiesis in aged mice, expands naïve T cells, and restores both vaccine and antitumour responses.5 The thymus, long treated as a vestigial organ that involutes and is written off, turns out to be reachable with an mRNA-lipid nanoparticle.

Human data had already begun undermining the vestigial framing. Patients who undergo thymectomy in adulthood carry elevated mortality, cancer and autoimmunity risk, correlated with reduced thymopoiesis and a pro-inflammatory cytokine profile.6 Removing the adult thymus is not free.

Capital followed. In July 2026 United Therapeutics acquired Thymmune — a Cambridge company building iPSC-derived thymic epithelial cells, previously funded by ARPA-H — for $140M cash plus up to $160M in milestones.7 In August, Tolerance Bio licensed a long-acting IL-7 for thymic indications in a deal reported at roughly $260M. In June, NewLimit closed $435M at about $3.1B on the strength of epigenetic reprogramming, and reports transcription-factor sets that restore youthful killing function to aged T cells.8

One half of the field just failed its biggest test. The other half just had its best year. Anyone claiming to know which way this resolves is guessing.

§ 04

And the measurement problem got worse, not better

ZEUS raised the bar for what counts as evidence. If hsCRP can be crushed without a clinical benefit, then every aging biomarker with weaker credentials than hsCRP is now on notice — which is nearly all of them.

The honest accounting: hsCRP is the only fully registrational enrichment biomarker anyone has. Clonal haematopoiesis is a validated enrichment biomarker, increasingly accepted by reviewers. Everything else — GrimAge, DunedinPACE, iAge, IMM-AGE, organ and cell-type proteomic clocks, immune resilience scores — has strong prospective epidemiology and no regulatory qualification.

Two 2026 developments make this tractable rather than merely depressing. Furman, Herzog and colleagues published a framework in Nature Medicine for which immune-aging biomarkers can serve as surrogate endpoints, and found that multidimensional functional scores outperform single analytes.9 And in April the Allen Institute released its Human Immune Health Atlas — 71 immune cell subsets across more than a hundred donors, with a companion resource of over sixteen million cells from donors aged 25 to 90.10 The reference data now exist. What does not exist is agreement on which measurement would change a regulator's mind.

§ 05

Why a dinner, and why this one

There is no shortage of conferences where people present finished work to a room that has stopped listening. The questions above are not settled enough for that format. They need the version of a scientist that appears after the talk, at the table, when the slide deck is closed.

So: seventy-five people, three Thursdays, in Cambridge. A keynote first, before dinner, so the room is primed on the right question rather than the speaker's favourite one. Lightning talks with a visible clock. A panel built on five questions that recur every night — modality, stratification, endpoints, biomarkers, trial design. Then dinner, with one of those questions printed on every table and someone at each table taking notes.

Talks are recorded and published. Everything from dinner onward is under the Chatham House rule, because the useful sentence — the one that begins “honestly, what we actually saw was” — does not get said on camera.

What comes out is a written playbook: five questions, three nights, and the field's best current answer to each, attributed to the argument rather than to any individual. If that document is any good, it is worth more than the dinners.

Nights one and two run in April 2027. The third — on tools, biomarkers and trial design — is added once funding clears. If you would like it to happen, that is what partnership buys.
